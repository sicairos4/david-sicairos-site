Beginning Is Not Finishing

The 2-Minute Rule separates starting a task from finishing it. You finish with one cue, one observable starting action, and a decision boundary.

Write Your Two Minute Start

A starting instruction is written in order: one cue, one visible first action, one boundary where you choose to continue, adapt, or stop.

REALIGNMENT / START

The 2-Minute Rule: define a small start

Separate beginning a task from finishing it. For this exercise, choose a first action small enough to try for two minutes or less. The point is a clear starting instruction, not a promise that a timer will remove every barrier. At the boundary, you may continue, adapt or stop. Essential work, access needs and rest still matter.

Worked example

Instead of “finish my proposal,” try “after I open my laptop, open the proposal document and write one heading.” When that small start is done, decide whether another step fits the time available.

One Outcome, Honestly Chosen

A priority is one outcome worth moving forward today. You finish with one outcome, one concrete next action, and one protected constraint.

Build Your Priority Page

Build the page in order: one outcome, one concrete next action, one protected constraint, written down before the day runs.

REALIGNMENT / DIRECTION

One Page, One Priority

A priority is an outcome you want to move forward, not a declaration that everything else is unimportant. Choose one outcome for the day, name a useful next action and acknowledge a real constraint. A smaller honest plan is preferable to treating every task as equally urgent. Required responsibilities and changes can still take precedence.

Worked example

Outcome: send a clearer project outline. Next action: write the three questions the outline must answer. Constraint: a meeting divides the afternoon, so reserve a short block rather than assume an uninterrupted day.

A Cue You Already Have

Habit stacking borrows an event that already happens in your day. You finish with a cue, an action, and a fallback, written as one sentence.

Write Your Cue, Action, Fallback

Write the sentence in order: one cue you already have, one small action, and one fallback for a changed day. You finish with your own cue, action, and fallback sentence.

REALIGNMENT / CUE

Habit Stacking Basics

Use an existing event as a place to consider a small action. Write “after this cue, I will do this action,” then add a fallback for a changed day. Choose a cue that actually occurs in your life, not an ideal schedule you rarely follow. This is an experiment in organization, not a guarantee that repeating a sentence creates a habit.

Worked example

After putting my lunch away, I will look at my next task. If I am away from my usual desk, I will make a brief note instead. A missed cue means choosing the next reasonable opportunity, not adding punishment or catch-up work.

Value, Action, Review

The Realignment Method connects one value to one small action to one review question, with no score attached. You finish with a value, an action, and a review question.

Run Your First Realignment

Pick the value, name the action, write the question you will actually ask at review. You finish with your first completed realignment.

REALIGNMENT / REVIEW

The Realignment Method: direction into action

For this exercise, connect a value to an action you can describe and a question you can review. You do not need to publish your identity or expose personal writing to use the framework. Keep the action small enough to fit current circumstances, and let the review inform a change rather than produce a score.

Worked example

Value: useful communication. Action: clarify the next step in one project message. Review question: did the recipient know what was needed, and what should I explain differently next time?

Input, Steps, Output

A workflow is work that enters, changes, and leaves. You finish with your input-to-output map, with every permission step marked.

Map One Real Workflow

Name the input, the actual steps, and the condition that makes the output usable. You finish with your map of one real workflow, permission steps marked.

SYSTEMS / MAP

Map One Workflow

Choose a repeatable piece of work and describe how something enters, changes and leaves it. Name the input, the actual steps and the condition that makes the output usable. Start with what happens now, including waits and handoffs. A map is not evidence that the process is automated, reliable or authorized.

Worked example

A newsletter draft begins with one reviewed idea, moves through writing and source review, and ends as a ready-to-review draft. Sending it is a separate authorized action. “Draft exists” and “newsletter sent” are not the same output.

Work Waits in Specific Places

A bottleneck is one named place where work waits, found by observation, not by blame. You finish with one named bottleneck, described by observation.

One Bounded Change, Then Watch

One bounded change you can make and undo, judged by the observation you chose in advance. You finish with an observation, one proposed change, and an observation to revisit.

SYSTEMS / OBSERVE

Find Your Bottleneck

Look for a specific place where work waits or repeatedly returns for clarification. Describe an observation before choosing a fix. The busiest person or longest task is not automatically the cause of every delay. Pick one bounded change and decide what you will observe afterward, without promising a productivity gain.

Worked example

Three drafts wait because no audience has been chosen. A small change is to write an audience sentence before drafting. The next observation is whether another draft still waits for the same missing decision, not whether the team “feels more optimized.”

Your bounded improvement

Your output: an observation, one proposed change and an observation to revisit. Keep a way to undo a change that makes the process worse.

A Draft Is Not a Publication

Systems for creators keep the route honest: idea, draft, review, a deliberate publishing action, and a record of the outcome. You finish with a draft-to-destination map with a decision boundary and an honest outcome record.

Draw Your Draft to Destination Route

Draw your own route, box by box: sequence, decision boundaries, and the receipt. You finish with your own draft-to-destination route, with its decision boundaries marked and its outcome record named.

SYSTEMS / CREATE

Systems Thinking for Creators

Give one piece of content a clear route from idea to reviewed draft to a deliberate destination. Name where you keep working material, where a decision is required and what receipt would demonstrate the final action. A saved draft, copied caption or opened share sheet is not proof of publication.

Worked example

Idea → master draft → platform version → source/privacy review → chosen publishing action → returned post link, if the destination provides one. If a route fails, preserve the draft and record the uncertainty rather than blindly repeat a possibly completed post.
