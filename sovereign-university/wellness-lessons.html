WELLNESS / HYDRATION

Hydration Habits

Water is one option for staying hydrated; other drinks and some foods also contribute fluid. Rather than prescribe a universal volume, this lesson asks you to notice a practical opportunity to drink regularly. Needs and medical instructions differ. If a clinician has prescribed a fluid allowance or restriction, follow that plan instead of adding drinks to meet a general target.

Worked example

A person without a prescribed fluid restriction chooses to keep drinking water accessible during a usual meal break. The small planning change is access and a recognizable cue, not finishing a large bottle or earning a streak. Someone following a fluid allowance would organize their existing care plan, not copy this example's drinking pattern.

Try one practical change

  1. Choose a familiar point in your day when a suitable drink is accessible.
  2. Choose a drink that fits your needs, preferences and existing advice.
  3. Notice practical barriers such as access, cost or a changed schedule. Do not use the worksheet to diagnose dehydration or to override medical advice.
Your hydration planning worksheet

Your output: one practical cue and access change, consistent with any existing healthcare instructions. This is not a personalized fluid prescription.

Sources: NHS: water, drinks and hydration · Milton Keynes University Hospital: prescribed fluid restriction.

WELLNESS / MOVEMENT

5-Minute Mobility: a gentle option, not a quota

Five minutes is an optional planning window that can include preparation, pauses and rest. It is not a required workout, a rehabilitation dose or a reason to continue through symptoms. The NHS provides illustrated seated movements that can be adapted to ability; if you are unsure whether they suit you, ask a health professional involved in your care before trying them.

Set up before moving

For the NHS seated examples, use a solid, stable chair without wheels and a position in which your feet can be supported. Move only through a comfortable range and build up gradually rather than forcing a stretch. Follow any activity restrictions. Stop if movement causes pain, dizziness or makes you feel unwell, and seek medical advice. Serious or sudden symptoms need urgent help rather than another exercise attempt.

Worked example

After reading the illustrated guide and deciding that the movement is suitable, a person chooses the NHS hip-marching example: gently lift one bent knee as far as comfortable, lower it with control and use the other side if appropriate. They may pause or stop well before five minutes. This example is not a required sequence or repetition target.

Your movement planning worksheet

Your output: a safe-to-consider setup, one appropriate option and a stop/rest boundary. Leaving the exercise blank while seeking advice is a valid choice.

Sources: NHS: illustrated sitting exercises and suitability guidance · North Bristol NHS Trust: seated exercise guidance and stopping if unwell · British Heart Foundation: chair-exercise precautions.

WELLNESS / REST

Sleep Wind-Down Basics

A wind-down is a transition toward your planned sleep period, not a task to perform perfectly. NIH/NHLBI guidance includes allowing enough time for sleep, keeping a reasonably consistent schedule and using quiet time before bed. Bright light, caffeine and alcohol can interfere with sleep. Choose one realistic change rather than turn rest into another demanding routine.

Worked example

After the last necessary task, a person writes one note for tomorrow, reduces unnecessary bright light where practical and reads quietly. They protect time for sleep instead of extending their to-do list. A shift worker can organize the transition around their actual sleep period rather than assume it happens at night.

Choose a manageable transition

  1. Name when you intend to begin winding down, allowing for work, caregiving and other responsibilities.
  2. Choose one quiet activity or one environmental change you can control.
  3. Notice what gets in the way. A routine does not diagnose or treat a sleep disorder; seek professional advice if sleep problems persist or affect your day. Do not stop prescribed medicine based on this lesson.
Your wind-down worksheet

Your output: one realistic transition cue, one small change and a barrier to address. No sleep score, supplement or guaranteed result is implied.

Sources: NIH/NHLBI: healthy sleep habits, including shift-work considerations · NIH: healthy sleep and ongoing sleep problems.

WELLNESS / FOOD

Protein Made Simple

Protein foods are one part of a varied eating pattern, not the whole plan. The NHS Eatwell Guide includes beans, pulses, fish, eggs, meat and other protein foods; dairy and suitable alternatives can also contribute. The guide considers balance across a day or week rather than requiring every meal to be identical. This lesson does not set a gram target, prescribe calories or recommend a powder.

Worked example

For one ordinary meal, a person considers beans or lentils alongside rice and vegetables, or an egg alongside bread and vegetables. They choose an option that fits availability, cost, preference and allergies. These are illustrative combinations, not a complete meal prescription or a claim that either fits every medical diet.

Plan one food choice

  1. Choose a familiar meal and identify a protein-containing food you already enjoy or can access.
  2. Consider the rest of the meal and practical needs rather than maximizing a single nutrient.
  3. Follow existing medical dietary instructions. A registered dietitian or other appropriate professional can help adapt general guidance to special dietary or medical needs.
Your meal planning worksheet

Your output: one practical food choice and preparation step, consistent with your needs. This worksheet does not diagnose a deficiency, prescribe a high-protein diet or require a supplement purchase.

Source: NHS: the Eatwell Guide, protein foods, dairy and adapting guidance to medical needs.

WELLNESS / PRACTICE

Foundations of Everyday Wellness

Use the preceding lessons to choose one practical foundation: suitable drinks, appropriate movement, time for rest or an accessible food choice. The exercise is to organize a manageable action, not to score your health or change every part of life at once. General guidance is not a substitute for your healthcare plan.

Worked example

A person notices that their evening task list regularly crowds out a planned wind-down. Their experiment is to choose a stopping point for one nonessential task and prepare a short note for tomorrow. The review asks whether the arrangement was practical, not whether a checkbox proves better health.

Build and review one small plan

  1. Choose one action that fits your circumstances and any healthcare instructions.
  2. Name a cue and one constraint such as access, schedule or energy.
  3. Choose a time to review whether the plan was workable. Adapt or stop it if unsuitable; do not use missed days as a reason to add catch-up work.
Your one-foundation worksheet

Your output: one action, one cue/fallback and a review point. Persistent or concerning symptoms belong with appropriate health care, not an automated lesson score.
